# Nate's Tutor Primer — How to Co-Work Math With Esme

A practical guide so you can sit beside her without accidentally making it worse. You're a generalist with strong math instincts but you learned algebra in the 90s. The math hasn't changed, but the *language* and *method names* have. This doc levels you up in ~10 min.

---

## Three Levels of Understanding (your preference, applied to *your* learning here)

### 🟢 Primary-school version (ELI5 for Nate)
Algebra 1 quadratics are about one shape: a **U-curve called a parabola**. Everything in these 13 topics is either (a) **building** quadratic expressions, (b) **factoring** them apart, (c) **graphing** their U-shape, or (d) **finding where they cross zero** (solving). That's it. Four jobs.

### 🟠 Sophisticated insider version
The semester compresses to a single dance between three equivalent forms of a quadratic:
- **Standard form:** y = ax² + bx + c — what you *get* algebraically
- **Factored form:** y = a(x − r₁)(x − r₂) — exposes the **roots** (x-intercepts)
- **Vertex form:** y = a(x − h)² + k — exposes the **vertex** (turning point)

Every topic in Esme's list is either a *conversion* between these forms, a *graph* of one, or a *solve* of "where does y = 0?" The four solving methods (factoring, square roots, completing the square, quadratic formula) are just different ways to find the roots — and they each have a sweet spot. The Quadratic Formula is the universal hammer, but the others are faster in the right situations.

### 🟣 Strategy / coaching version
The teacher's biggest gift here is the **focused list of 13**. That's a real act of pedagogy: skip the breadth, master the spine. Your job as tutor isn't to *re-teach Algebra 1* — it's to make Esme **reliable** at recognizing which of these 13 patterns is in front of her, and **calm enough** to execute a recipe she has on paper. The win condition isn't "she understands quadratics philosophically" — it's "she can score 80%+ on Mr. Mistry's test because she knew which tool to grab and her hand wasn't shaking."

For an ADHD + dyscalculia kid, **the recipes are the curriculum.** Comprehension comes from repetition with low stakes, not from a perfect explanation. Your superpower is making the room feel safe enough that she'll attempt the next problem.

## What's "New Math" (compared to your 90s algebra)

Esme's textbook is *Big Ideas Math, Algebra 1, Common Core 2019* — pretty mainstream. Most things you remember are the same. Here's what's different in **naming**:

| You probably learned it as… | Esme calls it… | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| FOIL | Multiplying polynomials (7.2) | FOIL still taught, but "box / area method" also taught as a more general version. **Use whichever she prefers.** Both work. |
| Reverse FOIL | Factoring trinomials (7.5) | "Diamond problem" is the visual aid. Two numbers in a diamond: top × top = bottom × bottom (product); left + right = bottom (sum). |
| AC method | Same name (7.6) | Used when leading coefficient ≠ 1. |
| Difference of squares | Same name (7.7) | Identical. |
| Vertex form | Same name (8.4) | y = a(x − h)² + k. **Watch the sign of h** — it flips. (x − 3)² means h = +3, not −3. |
| Standard form | Same name (8.3) | y = ax² + bx + c. |
| Factored form / intercept form | Both names used (8.5) | y = a(x − p)(x − q). The textbook says **intercept form** because the roots are the x-intercepts. |
| Completing the square | Same name (9.4) | Same procedure. Take half of b, square it, add and subtract. |
| Quadratic formula | Same name (9.5) | Identical: x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a. |
| Discriminant | Same name | b² − 4ac. Tells you how many real roots: positive = 2, zero = 1, negative = 0. |
| Zero product property | Same name (7.4) | If ab = 0, then a = 0 or b = 0. Foundation of "solve by factoring." |

### Notation changes you'll see
- **f(x) vs y:** Used interchangeably. f(x) = x² + 2 means the same thing as y = x² + 2.
- **Function notation in graphing:** "f(3) = 11" means "when x is 3, y is 11."
- **"Zeros" / "roots" / "x-intercepts":** All the same thing. Where the parabola crosses the x-axis. (Esme: this is just three names for one idea.)

### What's *actually* different (pedagogy)
- **Multiple representations:** Modern math teaches kids to see the same quadratic as table, graph, factored form, vertex form. You may have just memorized formulas. She's expected to flip between views. **This is good** — it builds robustness — but it adds cognitive load.
- **The "diamond method" and "box method"** are visual scaffolds. Take them seriously. Don't say "we never did it that way, let me show you the *real* way." That's the #1 way to undermine her.
- **Graphing parabolas by hand** (vs. just on Desmos) is still taught. Test will require it.

## The "Don't Say / Say Instead" Cheat Sheet

| ❌ Don't say | ✅ Say instead |
|---|---|
| "That's wrong." | "Where did you start? Let's walk back one step." |
| "Just remember the formula." | "Let's look at the formula together." (Open the doc.) |
| "We did it like this when I was a kid." | "OK, show me your way first. Then I'll show you a trick if it helps." |
| "Come on, it's easy." | "This one's tricky. Let me see what you've got." |
| "You should know this." | "Let's see if it's in your recipe card." |
| "Try harder." | "Want to take 2 minutes, then come back?" |
| "That's the wrong sign." | "Check the sign on this line — I want to make sure I'm reading it right." |
| "Why did you do that?" | "Walk me through that step." |
| "No, no, no…" | "Hmm — let's check that." |

The pattern: **externalize the source of authority** (the recipe card, the textbook), and **share the cognitive load** ("let me see if I'm reading it right"). You become her teammate, not her judge.

## The 3-Question Check (use after every topic)

Don't ask "do you get it?" — she'll say yes to make it stop. Instead ask these three:

1. **"What's the first move?"** (Tests recognition — does she know which recipe?)
2. **"Show me an example."** (Tests procedure — can she execute it?)
3. **"Where would this trip you up?"** (Tests metacognition — has she seen the trap?)

If she can answer all three, mark the topic ✓. If she can only answer 1–2, do one more problem together tomorrow before moving on.

## Dyscalculia vs. Concept Gap — How to Tell

Dyscalculia tends to show up as:
- **Mixing up signs** (− vs +) in transcription
- **Miscopying numbers** ("I wrote 73 but the problem says 37")
- **Forgetting "carry the one" type micro-steps** in arithmetic, even when the algebra is right
- **Slow recall of times tables** (this is huge; she may need to look up 7×8 every time)
- **Calculator typos** that surprise her

A **concept gap** looks different:
- She applies a method to the wrong type of problem ("I tried FOIL on this addition")
- She skips a step that's required (forgets to set =0 before factoring to solve)
- She can't explain *why* a step works
- She memorized one example and can't generalize

**Tactics:**
- For dyscalculia stumbles: **never penalize them**. Give her a calculator, give her a multiplication chart, slow her down, double-check transcription as a *routine* not a *correction*.
- For concept gaps: **return to the recipe**. Read it together. Do a worked example together. Then she tries one.

## When She's Frustrated

Signs:
- Pushing the paper away
- Heavy sigh, eyes go elsewhere
- "I'm so stupid" / "I'll never get this"
- Hyperfocused on one problem for >5 min without progress

Responses:
1. **Stop the math.** Right now. Don't push through. The math you do while flooded doesn't stick — it just teaches her brain "math = bad feeling."
2. **Name the feeling.** "This is frustrating. Yeah." (Don't argue with it.)
3. **Take a 5-minute body break.** Water, walk to another room, pet the dog, anything. Body has to physically reset.
4. **Come back to a *different* problem.** Specifically: come back to a problem you know she can do. Win a small one. *Then* maybe revisit the hard one — or skip it for tomorrow.
5. **Never end a session on a fail.** Always close on a problem she got right.

## Recognizing the 13 Patterns (the meta-skill)

The biggest test-day skill isn't *doing* the math — it's **knowing which recipe to grab**. This is the part most under-coached. Practice this directly with flashcards or by reading her a problem and asking *only*: "Which topic is this?"

Quick decision tree:

```
Problem has +, −, × of polynomials, no = sign?
  └── Topics 1, 2 (Adding/Subtracting, Multiplying)

Problem says "factor"?
  ├── Is there a GCF first? → Topic 3
  ├── 3 terms, leading coefficient 1? → Topic 4 (7.5)
  ├── 3 terms, leading coefficient not 1? → Topic 5 (7.6)
  └── 2 terms, both perfect squares with a minus? → Topic 6 (Diff of Squares)

Problem says "graph" or "find vertex / axis of symmetry / x-intercepts"?
  ├── Vertex form y = a(x − h)² + k? → Topic 9
  ├── Standard form y = ax² + bx + c? → Topic 7 (or convert)
  └── Factored form y = a(x − p)(x − q)? → Topic 8

Problem says "solve" (has = sign, find x)?
  ├── Factors easily? → Topic 10 (Solving by Factoring)
  ├── Looks like x² = number? → Topic 11 (Square Roots)
  ├── Looks like x² + bx = number (no c)? → Topic 12 (Completing the Square)
  └── Anything else / when nothing else works? → Topic 13 (Quadratic Formula)
```

**Tape this decision tree near her workspace.** Reading it during the test (mentally) is half the battle.

## Your Co-Work Defaults

- **Sit shoulder-to-shoulder, not across the table.** Same-team posture.
- **Keep your phone face-down.**
- **Pen for you, pencil for her.** (You both write — but she does the actual work.)
- **Time it.** 15 min is the contract. End on time even if she's hot. (Stretches contract trust.)
- **Photo the work** when done. Build a portfolio she can flip through for confidence.
- **Last 30 seconds: "What's one thing you got right today?"** Always ask.

---

## When You're Stuck Too

It's fine. Say so. "Let me look at the recipe — I don't remember." Then look at the topic file together. Model the behavior you want her to have.

If neither of you can figure it out:
- Symbolab will solve it step-by-step: https://www.symbolab.com
- Khan Academy has a video for literally every Algebra 1 sub-topic
- Photo to Mr. Mistry: "We're prepping for the make-up — can you confirm if Esme is on the right track here?" (He'll appreciate the engagement.)
